This week

5 developments in the gap between agentic AI's demos and what it can actually finish.

01 / Benchmarks

SemiAnalysis just gave “agentic AI” a report card — and the top score is 12%.

One sentence: SemiAnalysis launched GDPx on November 2, a benchmark spanning 11 professional domains — including chip design, quantitative trading, and legal research — built specifically to grade AI agents on real deliverables instead of static Q&A, and even the leading model, Claude Opus 5.5, completed only 12.0% of its tasks. GDPx is positioned as a successor to OpenAI’s GDPval, scored as an average of three attempts per task with every domain and data partner weighted equally. Gemini 4 Carbon placed second at 10.9%, and GPT-6 Astra third at 9.6%. SemiAnalysis says it plans new versions every three to six months, treating the leaderboard as a moving target rather than a one-time stunt.

Why it matters. If the industry’s newest, most rigorous agentic benchmark tops out around one in eight tasks, most “AI agent” pitches you’re hearing this quarter are describing a demo, not a deployment — a gap worth pricing into any plan to hand real work to an agent today.

The signal. SemiAnalysis flagged that built-in safety classifiers interrupted some task attempts from models like Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra, meaning even these scores could understate what those models can do unsupervised — more on that in story 04.

02 / Benchmarks

This isn’t the first benchmark to catch agents overpromising — Berkeley’s did it five months ago.

One sentence: UC Berkeley RDI’s Agents’ Last Exam (ALE), released in June, tested AI agents on real professional tasks sourced from hundreds of outside experts, and found the best-performing agent — Codex running on GPT-5.5 — completed only about 26% of them, with every frontier agent scoring near zero on the benchmark’s hardest tier. ALE and GDPx measure different things — ALE leans on tiered, expert-sourced professional tasks, GDPx on domain-partner-graded deliverables — but two independently built benchmarks, five months apart, landed on the same rough conclusion: agentic AI’s real-world completion rate sits closer to a quarter of tasks than the near-fluent impression a chat demo leaves.

Why it matters. Two separate research teams converging on similar numbers is harder to wave off as one team’s methodology quirk. If you’re evaluating an “AI agent” product for your own workflow, test it against a task you actually need done before trusting a vendor’s demo reel.

The signal. ALE’s own authors are explicit that it measures task completion, not a labor-market forecast — worth remembering before anyone turns 26% into a jobs headline.

03 / Enterprise

Companies are already pulling the plug on agent pilots before they scale.

One sentence: Gartner forecasts that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, citing escalating costs, unclear business value, and weak risk controls, while a separate Forrester-and-Anaconda study found 88% of AI agent pilots never reach production at all. The pattern lines up with the benchmark numbers above: companies are discovering in production what GDPx and ALE already measured in the lab. Gartner adds a wrinkle worth remembering — it estimates only around 130 of the thousands of vendors marketing “agentic AI” actually ship genuine agentic features, with the rest relabeling existing automation.

Why it matters. If you’re being asked to adopt, build around, or compete against an “agentic” tool at work, the base rate says most of those pitches are still automation wearing a new label — and even the real ones are failing to reach production at a high rate.

The signal. A broader 2025 MIT NANDA study found 95% of enterprise generative AI pilots showed no measurable revenue or cost impact within six months — the pattern predates agents specifically, and agentic AI inherited it.

04 / The fine print

The 12% score comes with an asterisk SemiAnalysis attached itself.

One sentence: SemiAnalysis disclosed that built-in safety classifiers in models like Claude Opus 5.5 and GPT-6 Astra interrupted some GDPx task attempts, meaning the published scores may understate what those models can actually do when unsupervised. That cuts both ways for anyone reading the leaderboard at face value: the headline 12% might be the real ceiling, or it might be an artifact of safety guardrails doing exactly what they’re designed to do. SemiAnalysis hasn’t published a classifier-adjusted re-run, so for now the honest read is a range, not a single number.

Why it matters. Benchmark asterisks like this one are why “AI scored X% on Y benchmark” headlines deserve a second look before they inform a hiring plan, a budget, or a vendor contract — the methodology section usually matters more than the leaderboard screenshot.

The signal. Cost told a similar story: Gemini 4 Carbon reached roughly 91% of Opus 5.5’s score at about a third of the per-task cost ($16.29 vs. $51.41) — a reminder that the “best” model on a leaderboard isn’t automatically the one worth deploying.

05 / Careers

The agentic-reliability gap just became one of this year’s fastest-growing job descriptions.

One sentence: as benchmarks and enterprises both expose how unreliable agentic AI still is, demand is climbing for people whose job is specifically to measure that unreliability — titles like “agent reliability engineer” and “AI evaluation and reliability engineer” are now live on company career pages. Postings from companies building production agents describe building LLM-as-a-judge pipelines, defining pass/fail criteria for agent tasks, and monitoring agents once they’re live — essentially productionizing the same kind of testing GDPx and ALE just did at a research scale. The title isn’t standardized yet, and with it comes no established level or pay band, but the work itself — catching an agent before it ships broken — is clearly real and growing.

Why it matters. If agentic AI is going to close its gap between demo and delivery, someone has to measure that gap inside every company trying to ship it — and right now almost nobody has that job title on their resume yet.

The signal. Separately, the AI-training gig economy that emerged to teach these models is already feeding this lane: platforms like Mercor are posting contractor roles explicitly tied to agent evaluation and reliability work, often above their general AI-trainer base rate.

FOWL prediction 26

By next year, every major AI lab will publish its own “agentic honesty” benchmark — and none of them will agree on a passing score.

GDPx arrived five months after Berkeley’s ALE, each graded differently, each with its own asterisks. Labs have an incentive to commission benchmarks that flatter their own models, so the real signal won’t be any single leaderboard — it’ll be whether independent evaluators converge on similar numbers the way GDPx and ALE already have.

3 things to do this week.

01

02

If you manage a team piloting agentic AI, log the real completion rate on real tasks this month, even informally — that record becomes your case for keeping or killing the pilot before Gartner’s 2027 cancellation wave makes the call for you.

If you’re weighing a next move, look at “agent reliability” or “AI evaluation” postings on Mercor, Handshake, or direct company career pages — the pay and demand are real even though the title isn’t standardized yet.
