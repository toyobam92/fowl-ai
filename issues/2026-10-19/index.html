If you read nothing else

OpenAI’s CFO made 2026 the year AI gets practical for business. The numbers tell a messier story: nearly three in four enterprises that deployed AI customer-service agents have already rolled at least one back, and Gartner expects more than 40% of agentic AI projects to be canceled by 2027. The common thread isn’t that the models are too weak — it’s that almost nobody built the monitoring to catch failures before customers do. That gap is now a hiring problem, not just an engineering one.

Nova’s signal

This is the SRE story again. Nobody fixes flaky software by waiting for smarter code — they build the pager, the rollback button, and the person watching both. AI agents skipped straight to production without any of it.

Nova’s call. If you’re evaluating an agent vendor, ask for their rollback rate and incident log before you ask about their benchmark scores.

This week

5 developments behind the gap between “adopting” AI and running it.

01 / Enterprise AI

OpenAI’s CFO just made 2026 about “practical” adoption. The data says otherwise.

OpenAI CFO Sarah Friar used a January blog post to name “practical adoption” as the company’s core focus for 2026, pointing to health, science, and enterprise work as the priority areas and noting 2025 revenue passed $20 billion, with annual run-rate revenue growing roughly tenfold since 2023. Ramp’s own spend data, cited by multiple outlets covering the post, found businesses spent more on OpenAI’s models in December than ever before, with growth outpacing Anthropic and Google, per ITPro’s coverage.

Why it matters. This is the story enterprise software is organized around right now: AI moves from demo to daily tool, and the vendor with the most enterprise revenue gets to define what “practical” means. The rest of this issue is what that word is actually running into on the ground.

The signal. Friar’s own framing puts health, science, and enterprise ahead of consumer products for 2026 — a bet that the harder, higher-stakes deployments are where the real growth sits.

02 / Enterprise AI

Nearly three-quarters of companies that deployed an AI customer agent have already pulled it back.

A Sinch-commissioned survey of 2,527 senior decision-makers across 10 countries, run in January and February 2026, found that 74% of enterprises that deployed an AI customer-communications agent have since rolled it back or shut it down. The rollback rate climbed, not fell, among companies with more mature governance — 81% — which Sinch’s own researchers attribute to better monitoring catching failures that less mature teams simply never saw, per The Register’s reporting.

Why it matters. This isn’t a story about weak adoption; these are companies that shipped the thing and then pulled it. That pattern — deploy, discover the gap, retreat — is a process failure, not a capability one, and it’s the clearest sign yet that “practical adoption” and “production-ready” aren’t the same claim.

The signal. 86% of the same survey’s respondents said they’re now evaluating or considering a different AI vendor — a rollback, in other words, usually isn’t the end of the relationship with AI agents, just with that one.

03 / Enterprise AI

Gartner says over 40% of agentic AI projects will be scrapped by 2027 — and most “agentic” vendors aren’t.

Gartner forecasts that more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by the end of 2027, citing spiraling costs, unclear business value, and weak risk controls. Analyst Anushree Verma’s underlying point is blunter: most agentic AI projects today are early-stage experiments, misapplied and driven more by hype than a working deployment plan, per CDO Magazine’s coverage. Gartner separately estimates that only about 130 of the thousands of vendors marketing “agentic AI” products are doing anything that actually qualifies — the rest is what it calls “agent washing.”

Why it matters. If the vendor pitch and the underlying product are frequently mismatched, due diligence on an “agentic AI” purchase has to start by asking what the system autonomously decides versus what it just automates — not by the word on the slide.

The signal. In a Gartner poll taken in January 2025, only 19% of organizations said they’d made significant investment in agentic AI; most were still conservative, undecided, or not investing at all — the cancellation forecast is landing on a market that was already more cautious than the hype cycle suggested.

04 / Enterprise AI

The share of companies abandoning most of their AI projects nearly doubled in a year.

S&P Global Market Intelligence’s 2025 survey of more than 1,000 enterprises in North America and Europe found that 42% now say they abandon most of their AI initiatives before they reach production, up from 17% the year before. The same respondents reported scrapping an average of 46% of their AI proofs-of-concept, with cost, data privacy, and security risk named as the top blockers.

Why it matters. A jump from 17% to 42% in one year means the honeymoon phase — pilot everything, worry about production later — is ending company by company, and the organizations left exposed are the ones that never built a path from pilot to production in the first place.

The signal. The survey’s own framing puts cost ahead of capability as the top-cited blocker, which lines up with Sinch’s and Gartner’s separate findings: the money problem and the reliability problem are, in practice, the same problem wearing different labels.

05 / Reliability

Amazon’s own AGI lab says the holdup was never the model.

At VB Transform 2026, Amazon AGI Autonomy director Bryan Silverthorn presented a framework arguing enterprises aren’t stalling on AI agents because the technology isn’t smart enough — they’re stalling because it isn’t predictable enough, per CryptoBriefing’s recap. His team breaks reliability into four measurable parts — consistency, robustness, predictability, and safety — and argues standard benchmark scores are a snapshot, not a reliability measure. One example from the talk: a QA agent read serial numbers correctly for two months, then started intermittently misreading them, traced back to a vision model that behaved differently depending on where the number appeared on screen, plus an invisible software change underneath it. A separate industry survey cited alongside the talk found that 85% of enterprises are piloting AI agents, but only 5% have gotten one into real production use.

Why it matters. When the team building the model is the one saying the model isn’t the bottleneck, that’s a strong signal that the next competitive edge in enterprise AI is the unglamorous layer around the model — monitoring, evaluation, rollback — not a smarter base model.

The signal. Only 4% of senior technology leaders in that same research said they’d trust model guardrails alone as a safety mechanism — nearly everyone wants a second layer of control outside the model itself.

From the community

Reported experience · r/AI_Agents and production-engineering teams.

When Sinch’s 74% rollback number circulated on Reddit’s r/AI_Agents, builders treated it less as news than as confirmation — many said it matched what they’d already lived through: an agent that worked fine in the demo and fell over the first time a deploy or a Kubernetes pod kill interrupted it mid-task. Engineers writing up their own agent outages keep naming the same root cause: no durable checkpoint, so a restart mid-run sends the agent back to square one with nobody paged.

FOWL prediction 22

By December 31, 2026, at least one hyperscaler will ship a built-in “agent reliability score” as a paid dashboard tier — not a free safety patch — turning third-party eval shops like Mercor’s new enterprise-evals line into acquisition bait rather than permanent outside vendors.

Amazon just spent a flagship conference session turning reliability into a named, four-part framework instead of a vague concern, and Gartner’s “agent washing” warning gives buyers a reason to demand proof, not vendor claims, before they purchase. The clearest business model behind that proof is a dashboard enterprises pay for, not a free checkup — the same pattern as this issue’s Opportunity Board, where reliability work has already become a billable service. The honest counter-case: if a high-profile agent failure forces a regulatory response first, a free, mandatory disclosure standard could beat any vendor to market.

Put it to work

3 things to do this week.

01

If your team runs or is piloting an AI agent, find out who gets paged when it fails — and whether that person has ever actually been paged.

02

Before trusting an “agentic AI” pitch, ask for the vendor’s rollback rate and what counts as a production incident; Gartner’s own estimate is that only about 130 of the thousands of vendors using the word “agent” are doing anything that qualifies.

03

If evaluation or reliability work looks like a career lane, Mercor’s Enterprise Evals Platform listings and Accenture’s AgentOps Engineer postings are a live, hireable description of exactly what this issue is about — read what the role actually asks for before assuming it needs a security or ML background.

Career radar

AgentOps Engineer

This is the job this issue’s reliability gap created: someone who keeps an agent’s accuracy, latency, cost, and uptime inside acceptable bounds once it’s live — and owns the rollback when it isn’t. Accenture’s AgentOps Engineer – AI Managed Services listing in Singapore spells out the job description almost exactly: observability, incident response, release management, and tracking token consumption as a cost lever. Mercor is hiring a Member of Technical Staff, Enterprise Evals Platform for the adjacent half — building the evaluation layer meant to catch a failure before a customer does — and a separate part-time AI Engineer – AI Trainer listing pays $155K for the same eval-infrastructure skill set. Titles and pay vary a lot by poster — treat the ranges as directional, not a market median.

A place to start. Learn to write an evaluation harness for a model or agent you already have access to — the skill underneath every role above — before worrying about the SRE or security credentials some postings list.
