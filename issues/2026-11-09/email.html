Why now

If you read nothing else

In September, the Third Circuit became the first federal appeals court to rule on AI training and fair use — and sided against the AI company, because its tool competed directly with its training source. That cuts against the comfort AI companies took from Anthropic’s and Meta’s 2025 district wins, neither tested on appeal. Anthropic’s $1.5 billion settlement, finalized in July, closed the industry’s biggest case without answering that question. More than 80 suits are pending, and the Ross ruling’s market-substitution logic is now what everyone argues over.

Nova’s signal

Every one of these rulings turns on the same unsettled question: did the output replace the market for the original, or just learn from it. That’s a product design question now, not only a legal one.

Nova’s call. Before you call any ‘AI wins on fair use’ headline settled, ask whether that AI’s output actually substitutes for what it trained on.

This week

5 developments in the fight over whether training AI on copyrighted work is legal.

01 / Courts

The first appeals court to rule on AI training and copyright just sided against the AI company.

On September 29, 2026, the Third Circuit Court of Appeals affirmed a district court ruling that Ross Intelligence’s use of Thomson Reuters’ Westlaw headnotes to train a competing legal-research AI tool was not fair use — the first time a federal appeals court has ruled on whether training an AI model on copyrighted material qualifies as fair use, per Plagiarism Today’s breakdown of the opinion and MLex’s reporting on the ruling.

Why it matters. Every AI company’s fair-use defense up to now rested on favorable district-court rulings that never survived an appeal — Anthropic’s and Meta’s biggest wins both settled or stayed narrow before reaching a circuit court. This is the first time an appellate court actually weighed in on AI training, and it went against the AI company.

The signal. The panel leaned hard on market substitution: Ross built a product that competed directly with Westlaw’s own business, which the judges found impossible to wave off as ‘transformative.’ Whether an AI’s output competes with the work it trained on is likely to matter far more going forward than whether the training process itself resembled how a person reads a book.

02 / Courts

Anthropic’s $1.5 billion settlement closed the industry’s biggest copyright case without ever answering the question everyone thought it would.

A federal judge granted final approval on July 20, 2026 to Anthropic’s $1.5 billion settlement with a class of authors — the largest copyright class-action settlement in U.S. history — covering roughly half a million works whose pirated copies Anthropic used to build its models, with payments running in installments through September 2027, per Pearl Cohen’s coverage and Jurist’s reporting.

Why it matters. Because the case settled instead of going to trial or appeal, the 2025 district-court ruling that training on lawfully acquired books was fair use — the ruling AI companies have been citing ever since — was never tested by a higher court. Anthropic’s own deputy general counsel has said that ruling is still ‘the law today,’ but that’s only true until a similar case reaches an appeals court and loses, which is exactly what just happened next door in the Ross case.

The signal. The settlement drew objections that the payout was too small relative to the works involved, and the court rejected all of them — a sign it treated this as a negotiated resolution to a liability problem (pirated sourcing), not a verdict on whether AI training itself is lawful.

03 / Courts

Meta’s fair-use win still stands — but the judge who wrote it warned against reading too much into it.

In June 2025, Judge Vince Chhabria granted Meta summary judgment, ruling that training its Llama models on books by thirteen authors, including Sarah Silverman, was fair use because the authors couldn’t show the training caused market harm. The case, Kadrey v. Meta, has continued through 2026 with an amended complaint and separate claims over distributing and uploading pirated files still being litigated, per case-tracking coverage of Kadrey v. Meta and reporting on the original ruling.

Why it matters. This is the ruling most ‘AI training is legal’ headlines point to, but Chhabria explicitly wrote that the decision doesn’t mean AI training is lawful in general — only that these particular authors, on this particular record, failed to prove market harm. It’s one data point, not a rule, and the surviving claims in the same case show the fight isn’t over even for Meta.

The signal. Market harm decided it for Meta the same way it decided Ross: the authors didn’t present evidence that Llama’s outputs competed with or displaced their books, which is close to the opposite of what Thomson Reuters proved against Ross.

04 / Courts

The UK’s first major AI-copyright ruling answered everything except the one question it was supposed to answer.

The UK High Court ruled on November 4, 2025 in Getty Images v. Stability AI that Stability’s Stable Diffusion model did not commit secondary copyright infringement, because the court found the model doesn’t store or reproduce the copyrighted images it trained on — but Getty had already withdrawn its core training-based claim during trial after conceding the training itself happened outside the UK, per PetaPixel’s coverage and DWF’s legal analysis.

Why it matters. This was billed as the UK’s first major ruling on AI and copyright, and commentators now agree it settled almost nothing about the central question: whether training an AI model on copyrighted work without a license is lawful under UK law. The jurisdictional technicality meant the court never had to decide it.

The signal. Getty did win a narrow trademark claim — the court found some instances of Stable Diffusion reproducing Getty’s watermark — but called them not ‘widespread.’ Getty has separate, still-pending claims against Stability in the US, where the training-location issue won’t apply the same way.

05 / Outlook

More than 80 similar lawsuits are still in line, and the next ones look a lot more like the Westlaw case than the book-training ones.

Ziff Davis’s copyright suit against OpenAI — consolidated into multidistrict litigation before a federal judge in the Southern District of New York — is still pretrial, with OpenAI arguing in mid-2026 that a recent Supreme Court ruling narrows the news publisher’s claims, while Disney, Universal, and Warner Bros. Discovery’s consolidated suit against Midjourney remains tied up in discovery fights with no ruling yet on fair use, per a tracker of the Ziff Davis v. OpenAI litigation and coverage of the Midjourney discovery dispute.

Why it matters. Unlike the book-training cases, these suits involve plaintiffs — news publishers, movie studios — whose core business is the exact kind of content the AI tools are generating. That’s precisely the market-substitution fact pattern that just lost in the Third Circuit. If that reasoning holds elsewhere, it’s a specific problem for OpenAI and Midjourney, not just a general one for AI training.

The signal. A related case worth watching for a different reason: Reddit’s suit against Anthropic over scraped user posts survived Anthropic’s attempt to dismiss it in 2026 on contract and unfair-competition grounds rather than copyright — a sign that platforms are reaching for whatever legal theory sticks when copyright law itself is this unsettled.

Signal & chatter

A few more things worth knowing.

Reddit’s suit against Anthropic isn’t a copyright case at all — and that might be the smarter play. Reddit sued Anthropic over scraped user posts using breach-of-contract, unjust-enrichment, and unfair-competition claims instead of copyright infringement, and a court let the case proceed through 2026 rather than dismissing it outright. If copyright law keeps splitting the way it has all year, contract and platform-terms claims may end up a more reliable path for plaintiffs than copyright itself.

From the community

Reddit and creator-forum reaction to the Anthropic settlement and the Ross ruling, not a vendor survey.

The reaction splits along a predictable line: skeptics keep asking how training on a book can be infringement when a person reading the same book wouldn’t be sued, while others point back to the actual distinction the courts have drawn all year — training on lawfully acquired work can be fine, but acquiring pirated copies to do it is a separate, clearly illegal step, which is the part that actually cost Anthropic $1.5 billion. A smaller but growing camp argues the honest fix isn’t a lawsuit at all, but a licensing system creators can actually get paid through.

FOWL prediction 25

By September 30, 2027, at least one more federal appeals court will rule on AI-training fair use — and the deciding factor will be whether the AI’s output competes with the training source, not how the data was acquired.

The Third Circuit’s Ross ruling, Anthropic’s and Meta’s narrower district wins, and the live cases against OpenAI and Midjourney all turn on the same fact pattern: does the output substitute for the plaintiff’s own product. Ziff Davis, Disney, Universal, and Warner Bros. Discovery are all suing over exactly that kind of direct competition, and some of those cases are far enough along to reach a circuit court within a year. The honest counter-case: appeals move slowly, and a company facing a Ross-style loss has every incentive to settle rather than risk setting more adverse precedent — which is exactly what kept Anthropic’s own case from ever reaching this point.

Put it to work

3 things to do this week.

01

If you’re building or using an AI product whose output stays close to its training source — summaries of news articles, answers that mirror a reference database — read the Third Circuit’s Ross opinion, not just the headlines about it; ‘does this compete with the original’ is now the question that actually decides these cases.

02

If you create or own copyrighted work AI companies might be training on, know that licensing marketplaces like Human Native and Created by Humans now exist as an alternative to litigation — getting paid for access doesn’t require winning a lawsuit first.

03

If you’re tracking this for career or business reasons, don’t treat any single ruling as the final word — four different courts reached four different outcomes this year alone, and the circuit split is the story, not any one case.
